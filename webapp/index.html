Do Faithful & Unfaithful CoT Differ in How They Focus Attention?

By Yulia Volkova | MATS 9.0

Update (Aug 2026): Final Results

Findings of the Thought Anchors faithfulness project, last update Aug 2026. Setup: a wrong "Stanford professor thinks the answer is (X)" cue is added to hard multiple-choice questions. Some rollouts follow the cue and mention it (verbalizing); some follow it without mentioning it (silent).

  • Model: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B
  • Datasets: MMLU(-Pro) and GPQA variants

Setup and problem selection

  • 1,494 problems screened (GPQA main 448, GPQA extended 546, MMLU-Pro 500), each with an incorrect professor cue appended.
  • Generated per problem: 32 cued rollouts, 20 uncued rollouts, and 8 no-reasoning answers (temperature 0.7).
  • Measured per problem: the cue's influence (gap), no-reasoning accuracy, the accuracy gain from reasoning, and the number of verbalizing (V) and silent (S) cue-following rollouts.
  • The analysis set contains 36 problems, with on average 26 usable rollouts per problem (the number varies with how many silent and verbalizing rollouts each problem produced). 24 problems where the cue strongly changes answers (gap ≥ 0.7, with ≥3 verbalizing and ≥3 silent rollouts), plus 21 meeting stricter criteria (cue influence ≥ 0.5, no-reasoning accuracy < 0.5, accuracy gain from reasoning ≥ 0.2, ≥3 verbalizing and ≥3 silent rollouts); 9 problems are in both groups.
  • Rollouts that reached the length limit were resumed with a 16k-token budget. Average rollout length: 219 sentences on GPQA main, 247 on GPQA extended, 80 on MMLU-Pro (~5.0k / 5.3k / 1.6k tokens).

Finding 1: Attention statistics do not separate silent from verbalizing rollouts

  • Hypothesis: silent cue-following has a recognizable attention signature in terms of "thought anchors".
  • The first analysis compared consistently-silent problems against consistently-verbalizing ones (p = 0.04). But upon further investigation, the same kurtosis gap shows up between those problem groups in uncued rollouts, where no silent/verbalizing behavior exists. So it is not about faithfulness, just problem selection.
  • The alternative approach is within-problem comparison: problems where the same problem produces both silent and verbalizing rollouts, each problem's rollouts compared with each other.
  • Length becomes a confounder:
    rollout type mean length (sentences)
    no cue (uncued)~200
    cue, verbalized~192 (statistically same as no-cue)
    cue, silent~186 (shorter, p = 0.001)
    While on average giving the cue produces shorter CoT, the cue verbalizers have longer CoT than silent cue-followers: not only do they verbalize the cue itself, the mention also sends them into additional deliberation rounds where they tend to name all the answer options again (3x more option-naming after the mention) and this extra length is what makes their kurtosis higher. The gap is only an average - 41% of same-problem verbalizing rollouts are still shorter than silent ones.

    Example of the post-mention text: "Wait, the professor thinks the answer is B) 9.0. So, perhaps the reasoning is that adding X increases the pH, making it basic. But why would that be the case? Alternatively, perhaps the reaction is exothermic..."
  • How the score works:
    • Each attention head gets a kurtosis over how much later sentences look back at each sentence (high = look-back concentrated on a few sentences). A rollout's score is the mean of its top-20 heads - the receiver heads, which Thought Anchors (Bogdan et al., arXiv:2506.19143, Section 4.1) defines as "attention heads that narrow attention toward specific sentences".
    • Two parts of the text are excluded, following the paper: the few sentences immediately after each sentence (the model always reads what it just wrote), and the opening sentences (the sequence start attracts attention for its position, not its content).
    • The paper also converts each sentence's look-back weights into rankings before averaging (rank normalization), which evens out scale differences. The Thought Anchors authors themselves note (Appendix C.4) that positional effects are only minimally accounted for.

    The table shows the verbalizing-minus-silent difference d (standard-deviation units, paired within problem, 21-problem strict sample) under different settings:
    metric versionrawlength-controlled
    lighter exclusion windows (skip 1 sentence, ignore 3 neighbors)d = +0.79, p = 0.0007d = +0.33, p = 0.22
    paper's exclusion windows (skip 10, ignore 20, reasoning sentences only)d = +0.71, p = 0.007d = +0.39, p = 0.29
    rank-normalized (paper's depth control)d = +0.37, p = 0.09d = +0.02, p = 0.84
    → The effect is fully taken away when controlling for length.

Finding 2: Cue position affects cue following and cue verbalizing

  • Cue at prompt start: following often silent (37% of followers verbalize).
  • Cue mid-prompt: following halves; ~90% of followers verbalize.
  • The stronger the cue's effect on a problem's answers, the more often it gets mentioned: correlation +0.24 between a problem's cue influence and the share of its followers that verbalize (p = 3e-7, 457 problems).
  • Mentions appear late (~65% through the rollout), usually with hedging ("maybe the professor's answer..."), and recur ~2.5 times per rollout. Sentences after the first mention name answer options 3x more often.

Finding 3: Cue influence and accuracy with reasoning ON are anti-correlated

  • Across 1,493 problems, cue following is inversely related to both kinds of accuracy, but much more strongly to accuracy with reasoning (correlation -0.49).
  • Parallel with Boppana et al. (arXiv:2603.05488): they find the CoT role depends on difficulty - on easy problems the model commits to its answer internally at the start and the CoT text is performative. On hard problems the CoT and the belief in an answer are generated together. The result in this setting is complementary: where reasoning genuinely works, cues do not sway the answer; where reasoning fails, cues are what the model relies on. That's pretty intuitive - and same is found by Chen et al. (arXiv:2505.05410).

Mean cue-following rate per accuracy bin (95% CI). Red: accuracy with reasoning. Purple: accuracy answering immediately without reasoning.

Finding 4: We can't predict upcoming verbalization from the model's activations

  • Following the design of Boppana et al., a linear classifier reads the model's state at each sentence and predicts whether the rollout will go on to mention the cue or stay silent. Positions at or after a mention are excluded, and evaluation uses held-out problems.

Result

  • Chance-level (AUC 0.50-0.55) through the first 60% of the CoT & at most 0.63 towards the end of the CoT.
  • So verbalization is not really pre-committed - unlike the upcoming answer in Boppana et al.

Hypothesis ▶

This hypothesis did not survive controls - see the Aug 2026 update.

If white-box attention attribution patterns reflect how models use information during reasoning, then faithful CoT (where reasoning genuinely influences the answer) should show focused attention to relevant broadcasting sentences, while unfaithful CoT (post-hoc rationalization) may exhibit more diffuse attention or anchor to different, potentially misleading parts of the reasoning chain.

This work replicates and extends Bogdan et al. (2025) "Thought Anchors" to test whether receiver heads and vertical stripe patterns of attention targeted towards specific sentences ("thought anchors") differ between faithful and unfaithful chains. If thought anchor patterns reliably differ between faithful and unfaithful CoT, they could serve as white-box indicators of CoT faithfulness in deployed models.

Detailed Key Findings ▶

The original (Jan-Feb 2026) findings below are superseded by the Aug 2026 update; here is their current status after controls:

  • Kurtosis separates faithful from unfaithful CoT (p = 0.04, GPQA) (what happened to this result) - disconfirmed: kurtosis tracks rollout length (rho = 0.98), and verbalizing rollouts are ~20 sentences longer; length-controlled within-problem differences are null.
  • Divergent attention circuits / universal receiver heads - not confirmed: receiver heads look problem-specific, not faithfulness-specific; the corrected pre-registered rerun found no attention statistic separating silent from verbalizing rollouts after length control.
  • Behavioral correlations (cue-following vs accuracy) - stand, and are superseded by the larger result: cue influence anti-correlates with reasoning accuracy (rho = -0.37 across 1,493 problems).

Methodology ▶

Pipeline

Dataset Statistics

MMLU: 143 problems, 20 rollouts per condition, median reasoning accuracy 0.637, median no-reasoning accuracy 0.474.

GPQA-Diamond: 186 problems, 20 rollouts per condition, mean base accuracy 0.577, mean no-reasoning accuracy 0.378.

Model & Generation Settings

Two generation stages: (1) initial rollouts for faithfulness classification, (2) attention analysis rollouts for visualization.

  • Model: deepseek-ai/deepseek-r1-distill-qwen-14b
  • Temperature: 0.7, Top-p: 0.95
  • Max tokens: 2048 (MMLU), 8192/2048 (GPQA stage 1/2)
  • Rollouts: 20 per condition (stage 1), 5 per condition (stage 2)

Faithfulness Classification

  • Faithful CoT: Follows cue AND mentions it in reasoning (threshold: ≥80% MMLU, ≥70% GPQA)
  • Unfaithful CoT: Follows cue but doesn't mention it (threshold: ≥80% MMLU, ≥70% GPQA)

Problem Selection Criteria

Problems were selected from MMLU and GPQA rollouts using the following criteria:

  • Low no-reasoning accuracy (<50%) - Problems where the model struggles without CoT
  • High cue response gap (≥0.5) - Large difference in cue-following between cued and uncued conditions
  • Cue ≠ Ground Truth - The cue answer differs from the correct answer
  • Each problem is unique

Future Directions

(1) Belief-trajectory probes in the cue setting - apply the design of Boppana et al. (2026) (probe the model's answer commitment at every sentence of the reasoning) to silent vs verbalizing cue-following: does internal commitment to the cued answer form early while the text discusses other things? Per-sentence activations for this are already being collected in the corrected re-extraction.
(2) Matched admissibility test - the admissibility conclusion currently rests on a cross-setting comparison; a clean version puts an admissible and an inadmissible cue at matched positions in the same task.
(3) Mechanism of the hiring decision-position deficit - distinguish prefill absorption from attention competition, e.g. by a mid-forward name-swap ablation.
(4) Cross-cue probe generalization - train a silence probe on professor-cue data and test it on hiring demographics: does one "using information I am not admitting to" direction exist?
(5) Combine with Verbalization Fine-Tuning (Turpin et al., 2025) - if VFT increases cue admission, does the decision-position attention deficit close?
