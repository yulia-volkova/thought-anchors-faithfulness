What attention does and doesn't tell you about a model following a hidden hint

Findings of the Thought Anchors faithfulness project — Yulia Volkova, Aug 2026. Setup: a wrong "Stanford professor thinks the answer is (X)" hint is added to hard multiple-choice questions. Some rollouts follow the hint and mention it (verbalizing); some follow it without mentioning it (silent). Model: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B; datasets: MMLU(-Pro) and GPQA variants.

Correction in progress (Aug 17). A bug was found in the custom attention kernel used for extraction: the library passes no causal mask to custom kernels, so the extraction forward passes ran with bidirectional attention (proof: saved normalized entropies reach 4.3; the causal bound is 1.0). Every attention-map statistic on this page — finding 2's measures, the layer-depth note in the conclusions, the probe inputs of finding 5 — is being re-measured with the fixed kernel. Separately, a cue-swap control has overturned finding 1's "absorption" exception: the 6 problems that kept following the hint under blocking do so even when the hint names a different answer, so that was the blocked configuration's own answer preference, not hint influence — blocking eliminates hint influence everywhere. Whether the blocking runs themselves need re-running under the fixed kernel is being checked. Behavioral results (screening rates, the position effect, the hiring decisions and reasoning texts) involve no custom kernel and are unaffected. The page will be updated as re-runs complete.

How the analyzed problems were selected

1,494 problems were screened (GPQA main 448, GPQA extended 546, MMLU-Pro 500), each with a wrong professor hint (hint answer ≠ ground truth). Per problem: 32 hinted rollouts, 20 unhinted rollouts, and 8 no-reasoning answers (temperature 0.7). From these we measured, per problem: the hint's causal influence ("gap" = how much more often the hinted answer is chosen with the hint than without), accuracy without reasoning, the accuracy gain from reasoning, and the number of verbalizing (V) and silent (S) followers. The analysis set is the union of two strata: strong hint causality (gap ≥ 0.7 with ≥3 V and ≥3 S rollouts; 24 problems) and the pre-registered strict stratum (gap ≥ 0.5, no-reasoning accuracy < 0.5, reasoning gain ≥ 0.2, ≥3 V and ≥3 S; 21 problems); 9 problems satisfy both, so the union is 36 problems, ~26 rollouts each. A narrower stratum (gap ≥ 0.7 plus positive reasoning gain; 12 problems) was also checked; all strata gave the same verdict on the attention statistics. Rollouts cut off by the token budget were continued at a 16k budget so verbalizers are not selectively censored. This selection ensures conclusions are drawn only where the hint actually changes answers and reasoning actually matters, at the cost of a small final sample.

1Blocking attention to the hint removes its influence

Causal test: during generation, the model is prevented from attending to the hint sentence. On 30 of 36 problems, hint-following drops to the same rate as when there is no hint at all. So the hint's effect on answers travels through attention to the hint — cut that connection and the effect disappears.

Exception: on 6 problems the model keeps following the hint even with attention to it blocked. There, the hint's content must be copied into the surrounding text's internal representations while the prompt is first read, so it no longer needs to be looked at. The most extreme such problem is also the one where the model almost never mentions the hint.

2No attention statistic distinguishes silent from verbalizing rollouts

The original hypothesis was that silent following comes with less focused attention. It doesn't hold. The concentration measure (kurtosis) mainly tracks rollout length; verbalizing rollouts are ~20 sentences longer, which produced an apparent signal.

Controlling for length (statistically, or by comparing only equal-length rollouts of the same problem) removes the difference completely. Same result on three dataset families and under pre-registered criteria. An attention-entropy measure gave the same null.

3Hint influence and reasoning ability work against each other

Across all 1,493 screened problems: the more a problem can be solved by reasoning, the less the hint changes the answer (rho ≈ −0.37 in each of the three datasets). One practical consequence: problems where the hint matters and reasoning also works — the population a faithfulness study needs — are rare (21 of 1,493).

4Hint position changes both following and mentioning

With the hint moved from the start of the prompt to the middle, following drops by about half, and silent following nearly disappears: mid-prompt followers almost always mention the hint. Following-without-mentioning is mostly a hint-at-the-start behavior. (The raw data of the original moved-hint run was lost to a disk cleanup, so the run was repeated from scratch with new random samples; it reproduced the original numbers — follow rate 21.9% vs 22% — so the finding does not depend on the lost data.)

5Detection works with labels, not without

A probe here is a simple linear classifier (logistic regression) whose input is the model's hidden state — the internal vector at one layer, taken at the end of the reasoning — and whose output is a prediction: silent hint-following or not. It is trained on rollouts whose label we know (from reading whether the reasoning mentions the hint) and tested on rollouts from problems it never saw. It reaches AUC ~0.86, meaning a randomly chosen silent rollout gets a higher score than a randomly chosen non-silent one 86% of the time. So the information "I am following the hint without saying so" is linearly readable from the model's internal state. But every label-free attention measure we tested — statistics you could compute without any labeled examples — failed to extract the same information. Practical consequence: if you can afford to label some examples, a probe is a workable detector; if you cannot, attention summaries are not a substitute. (Caveats: the best layer was chosen by test performance, so the exact number is optimistic; and the labels are keyword-based hint-mentions, an imperfect proxy for true faithfulness.)

Limitation of the probe result: the probe reads the state at the end of the reasoning, when the full reasoning text — any hint mention, the chosen answer — is already in the model's context. The label is a simple function of that visible text (that is how the labels were made), so a high AUC here mainly shows the text summary is linearly readable from the state; it is not evidence of hidden knowledge beyond the text. The informative version — probing before reasoning starts, predicting upcoming behavior — was invalidated by an extraction bug and not re-run; and since the pre-reasoning state is identical for every rollout of a problem, such a probe could at best predict problem-level tendencies, never which individual rollout will be silent.

6Related results on hiring decisions

Separate experiments on Karvonen & Marks' resume-screening data (their setting and logs, our attention measurement, Gemma-3-12B): the more a candidate's name changes the model's decision, the less the decision position attends to the name (rho = −0.63, n = 111 resumes). Follow-ups:

  • No "looking away". Measuring attention to the name from every position, the deficit exists only at the final decision position; while reading the resume, attention to the name is normal (early rho = −0.16 n.s., mid −0.04, late −0.01, decision −0.56). The model does not avoid the name; the decision position just does not consult it — consistent with the name's information being absorbed early (finding 1's exception) or with borderline decisions spreading attention across qualifications.
  • Same pattern for an inferred signal. With race signaled indirectly through college affiliation (Howard/Morehouse vs Georgetown/Emory), the inverse relation replicates: attention to the college name vs college-sensitivity, rho = −0.44 (p = 2e-6, n = 111). Weaker than names, but the model must infer the demographic here, so the effect is not about the literal name tokens.
  • Reasoning does not surface the bias. We generated new decisions with the authors' chain-of-thought prompt (8 samples per resume variant). The name bias survives reasoning at roughly one-fifth strength (carryover slope +0.18, p = 0.005); the college bias mostly disappears. Across all 7,392 reasoning texts, explicit demographic words appear zero times (the only string matches were "Six Sigma Black Belt" and "key man insurance"), and the college is named in 0.4%. The demographic cue sits mid-prompt — the position that made hint-following verbalized in finding 4 — yet following here is completely silent. Verbalization evidently tracks whether the cue is an admissible reason, not where it sits in the prompt.

Conclusions

Watching whether a model attends to a specific suspicious span is justified — that is how hint influence usually travels (finding 1) — but it misses the absorbed cases, which in our data were also the most silent ones. Watching attention statistics for unfaithfulness does not work (finding 2). With labels, activation probes are the practical detector (finding 5).

Exploratory observation, reported for completeness: receiver heads of silent rollouts sat slightly deeper in the network (about 1.5–2 layers). The effect shrank when the sample was widened (n=36→56, p 0.008→0.03) and fails the equal-length matched comparison (p=0.44), so we treat it as noise-level until shown otherwise. Method note: kurtosis-style attention measures are only comparable between rollouts of similar length; comparing them across groups that differ in length gives spurious results.
